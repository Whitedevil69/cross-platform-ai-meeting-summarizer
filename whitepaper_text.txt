A white paper on the AI-Powered Real-Time Meeting Decision Intelligence System
Author: Gaurav · B.Tech, Computer Science and Engineering · ITM University, Gwalior
Date: October 2026 Status: Proposal and design. No system has been built or measured
yet; all outcomes below are targets, not results.
1. Executive summary
Meetings are where decisions are made, yet the record of those decisions is usually a
recording nobody replays or a summary nobody verifies. Large platforms now ship AI
notetakers, and a crowded market of third-party tools works across Zoom, Google Meet
and Microsoft Teams. The transcription and summary problem is therefore largely
addressed. What remains weaker is trust: it is hard to see why an AI claimed that “Rahul
will submit the report by Friday,” whether anyone agreed, and whether the tool
understood a meeting that switched between Hindi and English mid-sentence.
This paper proposes a system built around four commitments:
1. One open Meeting Record. Every input, whether an uploaded file, a live microphone, a
browser meeting room or a connected platform, is converted into the same JSON
structure, so the AI pipeline is written once.
2. Evidence on every claim. Each decision or action item links to the transcript line,
timestamp and audio clip that support it. Unknown owners and deadlines stay empty
rather than guessed.
3. Participant read-back. Before a meeting ends, people confirm, edit or reject what the
AI understood. Only confirmed items become official.
4. Local-first processing and measured accuracy. Speech recognition and extraction run
on the user’s own machine by default, and the project publishes its own word-error and
extraction measurements on Hindi, English and Hinglish meetings.
On top of this foundation the system adds speaker attribution, a decision graph with
impact analysis, a browser meeting room, and optional platform connectors.

2. The problem
Decisions are lost. Participants must listen, speak and take notes at once. Action items,
owners and deadlines are the first things to disappear, and long meetings are expensive
to review.
Platform AI is tied to the platform. Reviews of the major suites note that Microsoft
Copilot’s notes work only inside Teams, and that Gemini’s notes can be unstructured free
text with little separation by speaker or topic [3]. Features are often bundled with higher-
priced plans [3].
Accuracy is uneven. Third-party analysis reports that Google Meet live transcription
struggles with heavy accents, technical jargon and overlapping speech [5]. Teams’ AI
Interpreter is described as depending on connection quality, accents and specialised
vocabulary [6]. A review of Zoom’s assistant mentions meetings that produced no
summary and no error message [1].
Notes are not accountability. A summary describes what was said, but does not check
who agreed, which earlier decision a new one replaces, or which tasks a changed deadline
affects.
Hindi–English meetings are a special case. Indian teams often mix languages within one
sentence. Vendors publish language counts, for example 60+ languages for Fireflies and
30+ for tl;dv [11], but a language count says little about how well a tool handles code-
switching. The sources reviewed did not publish accuracy figures for mixed Hindi–English
meetings.

4. Gaps and positioning
The landscape shows that capture, transcription, summaries and even cross-meeting
decision tracking now exist in products. This paper therefore does not claim novelty for
those capabilities. The following combination is, in the sources reviewed, not publicly
documented as a single offering:
A neutral meeting space where guests join from a browser with no install, with the AI
built into the space rather than attached afterwards, and not limited to meeting rooms.
Visible evidence and participant read-back as a core workflow, with confirmed,
edited and unconfirmed items kept distinct in the report.
Published, reproducible accuracy figures on Hindi, English and Hinglish meetings.
An open Meeting Record format that lets output from different sources feed one
decision graph.
Local operation alone is not a gap, because local assistants already exist [12][13]. Here it is
an enabler that supports privacy, while the differentiator is the combination above.
Statements of absence reflect the limited search done for this paper and should be
phrased as “not publicly documented,” not as proof that no product does this.
5. Proposed system
5.1 Design principles
Evidence before assertion. An item without a supporting transcript line is not shown
as fact.
Unknown stays unknown. Missing owners and deadlines are flagged, never invented.
Humans decide. AI output is a proposal until participants confirm it.
One format, many sources. All inputs normalise to the Meeting Record.
Local by default. Cloud services are optional and clearly labelled.
5.2 Architecture
The pipeline has six stages:

1. Input. An uploaded recording, live microphone or shared-tab audio, a browser meeting
room, or a connected platform bot.
2. Speech recognition. faster-whisper produces timestamped segments with per-
segment language detection [19].
3. Translation (optional). A multilingual model such as NLLB-200 translates segments
between Hindi and English.
4. Speaker attribution. In a browser room, each participant’s own audio stream identifies
the speaker. For mixed audio, a diarization pipeline such as pyannote.audio assigns
speaker labels after the meeting [20].
5. Extraction. A local language model, run through a tool such as Ollama, returns schema-
constrained JSON listing decisions, action items, owners, deadlines and open
questions, each with evidence.
6. Confirmation and reporting. Participants review items in a read-back step. The
confirmed record is stored and exported as a report, and optionally added to the
decision graph.
5.3 The Meeting Record
The Meeting Record is a small, open JSON structure. A simplified example:
{
"meeting_id": "m1",
"source": "upload",
"segments": [
{"id": 0, "start": 0.0, "end": 4.2, "speaker": "Speaker 1", "text":
"..."}
],
"items": [
{"id": "a1", "type": "action", "text": "Submit the report",
"owner": null, "deadline": "Friday",
"evidence": [{"segment_id": 0}], "status": "proposed"}
],
"edges": [
{"from": "a1", "to": "d1", "type": "depends_on", "status": "proposed"}
]
}
The source field records where the audio came from. The status field takes one of
proposed , confirmed , edited or rejected . The edges field supports the decision
graph. The structure is validated in code so malformed model output is rejected.

5.4 Evidence checking
The language model must cite the segment identifiers and exact words that support each
item. Code then checks that the quoted words actually appear in the cited segment.
Items that fail the check are marked unsupported or removed. This simple rule reduces
hallucinated tasks and makes every claim inspectable.
5.5 Participant read-back
Near the end of a meeting, or when the host chooses to wrap up, the system lists each
extracted item with its evidence: the transcript line, a timestamp and a short audio clip.
Participants select Confirm, Edit or Reject. The final report separates confirmed items,
edited items and unconfirmed items. Only confirmed items enter the decision graph. This
turns “the AI suggests” into “the people agreed,” and the correction rate becomes a
measurable quality signal.
5.6 Decision graph and impact analysis
Confirmed items form a small knowledge graph. Nodes are decisions, actions, deadlines,
people and meetings. Edges include assigned_to , due_on , depends_on and
supersedes , each with evidence and a proposed or confirmed status. The language
model may propose dependency edges, but a user must confirm them.
Three functions build on the graph:
Conflict flagging. When a new decision concerns the same topic as an earlier
confirmed one, the system shows both evidence lines and marks a possible conflict.
Impact analysis. If a decision or deadline changes, the system traverses known edges
and lists the tasks, owners and deadlines that may be affected, with the path and
evidence.
What-if queries. A user can ask what is affected if a deadline moves. The answer is
limited to known relationships and is labelled advisory, not a prediction.
5.7 Browser meeting room
A self-hostable WebRTC framework such as LiveKit or Jitsi lets guests join by link with
nothing to install. Each participant’s browser sends its own microphone audio, tagged
with a name, to the backend after an explicit consent click. This gives speaker identity
without diarization and avoids server-side audio mixing. The room is intended for small
meetings of a few people, not for competing with large video platforms.

5.8 Platform connectors
For people who stay in Zoom, Meet or Teams, a connector adapter converts platform data
into a Meeting Record. Two approaches are possible. A third-party bot API can join a
meeting from its link and stream transcripts back [17]. Native streams, such as Zoom’s
Realtime Media Streams, avoid a visible bot but depend on platform programs and their
maturity [18]. Connectors involve a third party, so they are labelled connected mode and
sit alongside the local default. They add convenience rather than novelty, since bot-based
notetakers across these platforms already exist.
6. Privacy, consent and responsible use
Consent first. Recording starts only after the host starts a session and each
participant accepts a visible recording notice.
Local by default. In local mode, audio and transcripts stay on the user’s device or
server. In connected mode, the interface states which external service receives data.
Retention and deletion. Users can delete recordings and generated artifacts
according to a configured policy.
Platform terms and law. Connectors must respect each platform’s terms and
applicable privacy rules, which should be checked before deployment.
No identity claims. Speaker labels such as “Speaker 1” are not real identities unless the
user assigns them.
Human oversight. AI outputs are decision support. The system never makes binding
decisions for participants.
7. Evaluation plan
The project will report its own measurements instead of marketing claims. Because the
dataset will be small, results will be described as a pilot study with error analysis.
Data. Eight to ten role-played meetings of five to ten minutes each, covering Hindi,
English and Hinglish, with two or three speakers. Each recording is hand-labelled with a
reference transcript and the correct decisions and action items.

9. Limitations and risks
Small local models are weaker. They may struggle with Hindi and with producing valid
structured output. Schema-constrained generation and early testing mitigate this, and
an optional text-only cloud setting can be offered.
Hinglish is hard. Whisper may output Devanagari or Roman script inconsistently, and
accuracy may be modest. Results will be reported honestly with failure examples.
Adoption. A new meeting space only helps if people use it. It is positioned for quick
meetings and guests, not as a replacement for existing platforms.
“Nothing to install” has limits. It applies to guests who open the link. People who
remain inside another platform cannot join the browser room directly.
Connectors depend on third parties. Their pricing, terms and capabilities can change,
and they conflict with strict offline operation.
Competitor claims. Statements about other products rely on public documentation
and may be incomplete.
Pilot-scale evidence. A small dataset supports indicative results only.
10. Conclusion
AI meeting notes are no longer scarce. Trustworthy ones are. The proposed system aims
to make meeting AI inspectable and accountable: every claim carries evidence,
participants confirm what becomes official, and accuracy is measured on the mixed
Hindi–English speech that real Indian teams use. A local-first design and an open Meeting
Record keep it independent of any single platform, while the decision graph shows how
one decision affects another. The work is an integration and evaluation contribution, and
its value will be judged by the measurements it reports.
